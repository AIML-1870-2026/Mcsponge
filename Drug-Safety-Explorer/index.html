How to Read This Data

A guide to interpreting the three data sources used in this tool.

⚠️ Interaction Warnings

Text from the FDA-approved prescribing label — the same document pharmacists and physicians use. Lists known drug interactions documented in clinical trials or post-market surveillance.

🛡️ Additional Warnings & Precautions

Black box warnings (the FDA's most serious alert) and general warnings & precautions from the same FDA label. Black box warnings indicate risks serious enough to require bold, bordered text on every package.

📊 Adverse Events (FAERS)

Voluntary reports from patients, doctors, and manufacturers. Key caveats:

  • Reports are not proof the drug caused the reaction.
  • Popular drugs have more reports due to higher usage.
  • Serious reactions are more likely to be reported than mild ones.

🔴 Recall History

FDA enforcement actions classified by severity: Class I (death possible) → Class II (reversible harm) → Class III (regulatory violation only).

🔍 Tips

  • Use generic names for best results (e.g., "ibuprofen" not "Advil").
  • Some older generics have limited FDA label data.

About Interaction Warnings

Source: FDA Drug Label (/drug/label.json)

What this data is

Taken directly from Section 7 of the FDA-approved prescribing label. Lists drugs, foods, and conditions that can alter the drug's effect or increase risk.

Limitations

  • Does not predict interactions in a specific patient.
  • Absence from the list doesn't mean it's safe to combine.
  • Labels may be outdated for older drugs.

Next steps

Use as a starting point for a conversation with your pharmacist or doctor — never as a replacement for professional advice.

About Additional Warnings

Source: FDA Drug Label (/drug/label.json)

Black Box Warnings

The FDA's most serious warning level. Required when there is reasonable evidence of a serious hazard and the benefit-to-risk ratio must be carefully considered. The name comes from the black border surrounding the text on drug packaging.

Warnings & Precautions

Section 5 of the FDA label. Lists serious adverse reactions and safety concerns that may require monitoring, dose adjustment, or discontinuation. Less severe than black box warnings but still clinically significant.

Important reminder

These warnings describe population-level risks observed during trials and post-market surveillance. They do not predict individual outcomes. Always consult a healthcare provider before changing any medication.

About Adverse Event Data

Source: FDA FAERS (/drug/event.json)

What FAERS is

The world's largest pharmacovigilance database — voluntary safety reports from patients, healthcare professionals, and manufacturers worldwide.

Critical limitations

  • Correlation ≠ causation: A report doesn't prove the drug caused the event.
  • Volume bias: Widely prescribed drugs have more reports by default.
  • Reporting bias: Severe or unusual reactions are over-represented.
  • Duplicate reports: The same event may appear multiple times.
  • Underreporting: Estimated 1–10% of reactions are ever submitted.

How to interpret the chart

Higher bars = more reports, not higher risk. Compare patterns within a drug, not absolute counts between drugs.

About Recall Classifications

Source: FDA Enforcement Reports (/drug/enforcement.json)

Class I — Most Serious

Reasonable probability of serious adverse health consequences or death.

Example: Drug contaminated with a toxic substance, or wrong active ingredient.

Class II — Moderate

May cause temporary or medically reversible adverse consequences.

Example: Labeling error causing confusion but unlikely to cause lasting harm.

Class III — Least Serious

Unlikely to cause adverse health consequences, but violates FDA regulations.

Example: Minor packaging defects or administrative violations.

Important note

Past recalls don't mean a drug is currently unsafe. Most recalled lots have been removed from market. Check FDA.gov for current safety alerts.
